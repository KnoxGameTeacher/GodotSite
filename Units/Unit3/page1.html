Section 1: Game Design Document

Plan your game using a Game Design Document. Learn about Minimum Viable Product (MVP) and how to avoid scope creep.

What is a GDD?

A Game Design Document (GDD) is a living document that outlines the vision, mechanics, and scope of a game. It serves as:

  • A blueprint for developers
  • A reference during production
  • A tool for communicating ideas

Think of it like a recipe: you wouldn't bake a cake without knowing the ingredients and steps. A GDD is your recipe for building a game.

💡 Why This Matters: A GDD helps you stay focused, organized, and on track. It's also a great tool for getting feedback from others before you start coding.

What is an MVP?

An MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is the simplest version of your game that still functions as a complete, playable experience. It contains only the essential features needed for the core loop to work.

Let's look at an example everyone knows: Minecraft.

Minecraft's MVP would include:
  • Player can walk and jump in a 3D world
  • Breaking and placing blocks (just dirt, stone, and wood)
  • Day and night cycle (simple, no sleep mechanic)
  • Hostile mobs (just zombies that appear at night)
  • A crafting table with a few basic recipes
  • A simple health bar
  • A survival goal: build a shelter and survive one night
What would NOT be in Minecraft's MVP:
  • Nether or End dimensions
  • Redstone and complex circuits
  • Enchanting and potions
  • Villages and trading
  • Multiplayer
  • Creative mode

The MVP of Minecraft would still be recognizable as Minecraft—you can walk, build, and survive—but it would be missing many of the features we know today. Those features were added over time as updates and expansions.

💡 Key Idea: "Make something simple that works before making something complex that breaks." Your MVP should be a game you'd be proud to show someone, even if it's not finished yet.

What is Scope Creep?

Scope creep is when a project grows beyond its original goals, adding features that delay completion and make the game harder to finish.

Examples of Scope Creep:

  • "Let's add 10 different animals!" (started with 3)
  • "What if there was crafting too?" (not in original plan)
  • "Let's make it multiplayer!" (was single-player)
  • "We should add day/night cycles and weather!" (beyond scope)
⚠️ Warning: Scope creep is the enemy of finished games. The more features you add, the less likely you are to complete your project.
💡 Pro Tip: The best way to avoid scope creep is to write down your MVP and stick to it. If you think of a cool new feature, add it to a "Stretch Goals" list for later.

Your Game: Forage Fever

You're going to create a game called Forage Fever. Here's the core concept:

  • You play as a creature in a 3D forest
  • You must hunt animals for food to survive
  • You have a hunger meter that decreases over time
  • Animals also hunt YOU
  • You need to balance hunting and being hunted

Your first task is to plan your version of this game. You get to make choices about:

  • What animals exist in your ecosystem?
  • What tools do you use to hunt?
  • What does the environment look like?
💡 Remember: You're building YOUR version of this game. Make choices that excite you!

MVP vs Stretch Goals

When designing your GDD, separate features into two categories. Think of it like planning your own version of Minecraft:

✅ MVP (Must Have)
  • Player can move and look around
  • Basic 3D world with ground and objects
  • Hunger meter that decreases over time
  • Health meter
  • At least 1 prey animal to hunt
  • At least 1 predator animal to avoid
  • A tool to hunt with (spear, bow, etc.)
  • Win condition (survive X time or collect X food)
  • Lose condition (health reaches 0)
🌟 Stretch Goals (Nice to Have)
  • More than 2 animal types
  • Crafting system for tools
  • Day/night cycle
  • Multiple environments (forest, desert, snow)
  • Sound effects and music
  • Polished UI with animations
  • Animal breeding or taming
  • Score tracking or achievements

Just like Minecraft started simple and added features over time, your game should start with the MVP and grow from there.

💡 Key Idea: Complete your MVP FIRST. If you have time, add stretch goals. A finished MVP is better than an unfinished game with 10 features.
Figure 1.6: Start with your MVP, then add stretch goals later

Share & Feedback

Share your GDD with a partner or in a small group. Give each other feedback on:

  • MVP Focus: Is the MVP small enough to finish?
  • Scope Check: Are there too many features for the timeline?
  • Clarity: Is the game concept easy to understand?
  • Excitement: Does this sound like a fun game to play?

Use this feedback to revise your GDD before you start coding.

💡 Pro Tip: Getting feedback early saves you from building something that isn't fun or is too complex to finish. Share early, share often!
