A Different Approach to GDD

In Unit 3, you learned how to make a Game Design Document. You learned about MVP, scope creep, and how to structure a plan.

In Unit 4, we're going to take that a step further. Instead of filling in a template, you'll learn to think like a designer — making deliberate decisions about what your game is and what it isn't.

These skills apply to any game you make in the future, not just Blocky Blocks.

What You'll Do This Time

  • Define design pillars that guide every decision
  • Analyze reference games to learn from existing tower defense games
  • Fill in a genre-specific GDD that asks harder questions
  • Use a prioritization matrix to deliberately cut features
  • Plan playtesting questions before you build
  • Prepare for iteration with a Change Log
💡 Key Idea: A GDD isn't a form to fill out. It's a thinking tool that helps you make better decisions before you spend hours building the wrong thing.
2

What Are Design Pillars?

Design pillars are 2-3 short statements that define what your game is about. Every design decision you make should support at least one pillar.

Examples from real games:

  • Plants vs Zombies: "Accessible to anyone, satisfying to master"
  • Bloons TD: "Endless waves, endless strategies"
  • Kingdom Rush: "Heroes matter, choices matter"

Write Your Own Pillars

Take a few minutes to write 2-3 design pillars for your Blocky Blocks tower defense game. Think about:

  • What should the player feel while playing?
  • What makes your version of the game unique?
  • What is the core loop and what makes it satisfying?

Write them down somewhere you can refer back to (your GDD document).

💡 Pro Tip: If you're ever unsure whether to add a feature, check it against your pillars. If it doesn't support at least one pillar, cut it. This keeps your game focused.

3

Why Analyze References?

Before you make your own game, look at 1-2 games in the same genre. Ask why they made certain choices — not just what they did. This is how professional designers plan.

Some tower defense games you can analyze:

  • Plants vs Zombies — Accessible, lane-based defense
  • Bloons TD — Endless waves, upgrade paths
  • Kingdom Rush — Heroes, hand-drawn art style
  • Sanctum — 3D first-person tower defense

You can also browse tower defense games on Itch.io or watch gameplay videos on YouTube.

Fill In This Reference Table

Pick a tower defense game and analyze it. Write this into your GDD.

  • What I Like: Mechanics, pacing, or features that appeal to you
  • What I'd Change: Things that could be improved or weren't satisfying
  • What I'll Borrow: Ideas you want to adapt into your own game
💡 Key Idea: You don't need to reinvent the wheel. Games in the same genre share mechanics because they work. Uniqueness comes from your pillars and polish.

4

Build Your Own GDD

You had experience with a GDD template from Unit 3, but now you'll adapt it for your tower defense game.

You can either make your own or use the template from last unit to get started.

If you use the template, you'll need to add the sections below into it:

Key Sections to Fill Out

  • Tower Design — How many towers? What makes each unique? (Fast, splash, sniper?)
  • Enemy Design — What types of enemies? (Standard, fast, armored, flying)
  • Wave Design — How do waves increase in difficulty? How does it scale infinitely?
  • Economy — How much gold per kill? How much do towers cost?
  • Path Design — What does the level look like? Single path, branching, maze?
💡 Pro Tip: The more specific your GDD, the fewer surprises you'll hit while coding. Answering these questions now saves you hours later.
Figure 1.4: A genre-specific GDD asks the right questions
5

Make Deliberate Cuts

Not all features are equal. A prioritization matrix helps you decide what's essential and what can wait.

Sort every feature into one of these categories:

  • Must Have — Your MVP. The game doesn't work without it.
  • Should Have — Stretch goals. Add if time allows.
  • Could Have — Nice ideas for a future version.
  • Won't Have — Not this project. Be honest about what you're cutting.

Fill in your own matrix in your GDD document.

💡 Pro Tip: Most students try to build too much. A game with 3 well-polished features is better than one with 8 half-finished ones.
Figure 1.5: Sorting features by priority helps you scope realistically
6

Plan What You Need to Learn

Before you spend weeks building a game, think about the questions you'll need answered. Playtesting isn't just for when the game is done — testing early saves you time.

Good playtesting questions for a tower defense game:

  • Is the game too hard or too easy?
  • Are any towers useless or overpowered?
  • Does the player feel rushed or bored?
  • Is the reward loop satisfying?
  • Does the player understand how to play without being told?

Write Your Own Playtesting Questions

In your GDD, write 5 questions you want to answer through playtesting. For each, note how you'll test it (small prototype, peer playtest, self-test, etc.).

💡 Key Idea: Playtesting isn't a final step. It's an ongoing process. You can playtest a rough prototype with placeholder graphics and still learn a lot.
Figure 1.6: Playtesting answers questions you can't answer alone
7

Your GDD Will Change

This is not a failure — it's expected. Every game goes through revisions as you learn what works and what doesn't.

Add a Change Log at the bottom of your GDD document. Every time you change something significant, note:

  • The date of the change
  • What you changed
  • Why you changed it

This is exactly how professional studios track their own design decisions.

Start Your Change Log

For now, add the first entry: the date you created this GDD and a note that it's the initial version.

💡 Pro Tip: A GDD isn't a contract. It's a plan that helps you know when you're off track — and lets you look back and understand why you changed direction.
Figure 1.7: A change log tracks how your design evolves
8

Get Feedback on Your Design Thinking

Share your GDD with a partner and have them answer these questions. The goal isn't spelling or formatting — it's whether your design decisions make sense.

Peer Review Questions

  • Can you tell what this game is about in one sentence?
  • What are the design pillars? Do they make sense together?
  • What feature is the riskiest? Why?
  • What would you cut if you had to?
  • What's the strongest part of this design?

Use the feedback to revise your GDD before moving on. Make sure to log any changes in your Change Log.

💡 Key Idea: Designers are often too close to their own work to see its flaws. An outside perspective catches problems you'd miss.
Figure 1.8: Peer review focuses on design thinking, not formatting
